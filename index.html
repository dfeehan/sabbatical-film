42 weeks · 24 Aug 2026 – 4 Jul 2027 · 85 core films, ~127 with extensions

How to use this

Each week has two core films and one optional extension. Two is the floor; take the extension in weeks when you have the time or the unit has its hooks in you. That lands you at 2–3 films a week as planned, without the schedule becoming a debt you owe.

Four weeks list three or four core films rather than two. In every case the extras are short — 14 to 32 minutes — and are marked as such. They’re there because avant-garde and documentary work is where the formal vocabulary gets isolated most clearly, and skipping it entirely would leave a real hole. A 28-minute film is not a third film; it’s an appendix to the one before it.

Films are grouped into 13 units of 3–4 weeks. Within a unit, the films talk to each other — an influence, a rebuttal, a second attempt at the same problem. This is the whole design. A single Antonioni film is a slog; three Antonioni-adjacent films in a row and you start to see what he was actually doing, which is when it gets interesting.

Three rules that matter more than the list:

  1. One film per sitting, no phone. These were built for uninterrupted attention and collapse without it.
  2. Write 3–4 sentences after each one — before you read anything about it. What did the camera do? What surprised you? What annoyed you? You know better than most that unrecorded observation evaporates.
  3. Read the criticism after, never before. One piece, ten minutes. Unit-level reading is listed below; the Reading Companion has linked essays for every individual film.

Runtimes are listed after the director. They’re for the standard version — the Criterion release where one exists, otherwise the most widely circulating restoration. Silents and older restorations vary by a few minutes depending on projection speed and transfer; where a film exists in genuinely different cuts (Apocalypse Now, Andrei Rublev, Metropolis), the entry says which one is meant.

Where to find these: the Criterion Channel covers a large majority of this list and is the single best subscription for the project. MUBI, Kanopy (free with most university logins — check Berkeley’s), and your library fill most of the rest.

Optional scaffolding: Bordwell & Thompson, Film Art: An Introduction. Read chapters 4–6 (mise-en-scène, cinematography, editing) during Unit 1. It’s about 100 pages and it gives you vocabulary you’ll use for the remaining 40 weeks. This is the closest thing to the class you never took.

If you fall behind: don’t catch up. Drop the extensions, drop a week, rejoin at the next unit boundary. The units are self-contained on purpose.

Unit 1 · The Grammar

Weeks 1–3 · 24 Aug – 13 Sep 2026

Six films chosen because each one makes a formal technique impossible to miss. Watch these with Bordwell open. The goal isn’t to love them — it’s to leave with words for what you’re seeing, so that everything after lands differently.

Week 1 · Aug 24–30

  • Citizen Kane (1941, Welles · 119 min) — deep focus, and a film that narrates itself in fragments. The whole vocabulary of “showy” filmmaking starts here.
  • Rear Window (1954, Hitchcock · 112 min) — the purest demonstration of how editing constructs meaning. Two shots, one implication, no dialogue.
  • Optional: North by Northwest (1959, Hitchcock · 136 min) — the crop-duster sequence is the standard textbook example of how editing builds tension out of nothing. Hitchcock appears in these academic syllabi more than any other director, and this is the film they teach.

Week 2 · Aug 31 – Sep 6

  • Do the Right Thing (1989, Lee · 120 min) — color, heat, and camera height as argument. Notice the Dutch angles arrive before the violence does.
  • Chungking Express (1994, Wong Kar-wai · 102 min) — step-printing, handheld, and the discovery that a film’s texture can carry its emotion entirely.
  • Optional: Killer of Sheep (1978, Burnett · 80 min)

Week 3 · Sep 7–13

  • Rashomon (1950, Kurosawa · 88 min) — the same events, four times. Watch how the camera’s behavior changes with each teller.
  • The 400 Blows (1959, Truffaut · 99 min) — the long take and the freeze frame; how withholding a cut creates unease.
  • Optional: Bicycle Thieves is coming in Unit 4 — instead try A Man Escaped (1956, Bresson · 101 min)

Read: Roger Ebert’s Great Movies essay on Citizen Kane; Bordwell & Thompson chs. 4–6.

Unit 2 · The Studio System

Weeks 4–7 · 14 Sep – 11 Oct 2026

Classical Hollywood at full operating efficiency. These are, without qualification, fun — which is the point. Genre is a set of constraints, and watching great directors work inside constraints is the fastest way to see directorial choice at all. Pay attention to how much these films accomplish in 95 minutes.

Week 4 · Sep 14–20 — Screwball

  • It Happened One Night (1934, Capra · 105 min)
  • His Girl Friday (1940, Hawks · 92 min) — the dialogue overlaps by design. Don’t fight it.
  • Optional: The Lady Eve (1941, Sturges · 94 min)

Week 5 · Sep 21–27 — The Western

  • Red River (1948, Hawks · 127 min)
  • The Searchers (1956, Ford · 119 min) — the most influential and most morally uncomfortable American film of its decade. The doorway shots bookend it for a reason.
  • Optional: Stagecoach (1939, Ford · 96 min)

Week 6 · Sep 28 – Oct 4 — Noir

  • Double Indemnity (1944, Wilder · 107 min)
  • The Third Man (1949, Reed · 104 min) — noir style exported to bombed-out Vienna, with a zither.
  • Optional: Out of the Past (1947, Tourneur · 97 min)

Week 7 · Oct 5–11 — Hollywood on itself

  • Singin’ in the Rain (1952, Donen & Kelly · 103 min)
  • Sunset Boulevard (1950, Wilder · 110 min) — the same subject, the sound-era transition, told as a horror film. Watch these back to back and the pairing does the work.
  • Optional: The Bad and the Beautiful (1952, Minnelli · 118 min)

Read: Manny Farber, “Underground Films” (1957) — his case for Hawks, Walsh, and Mann as unpretentious craftsmen, and against exactly the prestige-Ford register of The Searchers. Useful precisely because it argues with this week’s assignments.

Unit 3 · Silents, the Short Version

Weeks 8–10 · 12 Oct – 1 Nov 2026

You said silents are less appealing, so this unit is deliberately small and front-loaded with pleasure. Note the runtimes — every core film in this unit is under 95 minutes, and four are under 70. Keaton first, because Sherlock Jr. is 45 minutes of the most inventive physical filmmaking ever committed, and it will recalibrate your expectations before you meet Dreyer.

Weeks 9 and 10 carry an extra title each. Week 9 adds German Expressionism, which the plan otherwise skipped entirely and which every film-history course treats as foundational — and which Unit 2’s noir week is directly descended from. Week 10 adds two very short avant-garde films for the same reason: they isolate what narrative films do invisibly.

Week 8 · Oct 12–18 — Keaton

  • Sherlock Jr. (1924, Keaton · 45 min) — a projectionist walks into the movie he’s showing. In 1924.
  • The General (1926, Keaton · 79 min)
  • Optional: City Lights (1931, Chaplin · 87 min)

Week 9 · Oct 19–25 — Expressionism and the image at full power

  • The Cabinet of Dr. Caligari (1920, Wiene · 77 min) — painted shadows, tilted walls, a set that behaves like a state of mind. Watch it and Week 6’s noir stops looking like an American invention.
  • Sunrise (1927, Murnau · 94 min) — Murnau brings the Expressionist toolkit to Hollywood; the camera moves like nothing else would for thirty years.
  • The Passion of Joan of Arc (1928, Dreyer · 82 min) — almost entirely faces. It is not a comfortable film, but it is a short one.
  • Optional: Metropolis (1927, Lang · 148 min) or Nosferatu (1922, Murnau · 94 min)

Week 10 · Oct 26 – Nov 1 — Montage and the avant-garde

  • Man with a Movie Camera (1929, Vertov · 68 min) — a city symphony and a film about filmmaking; no plot, no intertitles, endlessly watchable.
  • Battleship Potemkin (1925, Eisenstein · 75 min) — read the Odessa Steps sequence as the argument for editing that everything after is answering.
  • Un Chien Andalou (1929, Buñuel & Dalí · 16 min) — Surrealism’s demonstration that film can dispense with continuity entirely and still hold you.
  • Meshes of the Afternoon (1943, Deren · 14 min) — the most-taught avant-garde film there is; it appears in more of the academic syllabi than His Girl Friday. Watch it twice — it’s fourteen minutes.
  • Optional: The Crowd (1928, Vidor · 98 min)

Four films, but the last two total half an hour. Real week runtime: about 2h 50m.

Read: David Bordwell’s blog, “Sherlock Jr.” posts; Eisenstein’s short essay “A Dialectic Approach to Film Form” (skim — the theory is dense, the examples are clear).

Unit 4 · Italy: Rubble to Ennui

Weeks 11–13 · 2 – 22 Nov 2026

Postwar Italian cinema runs an arc in fifteen years from filming actual bombed streets with non-actors to filming rich people being bored in beautiful rooms. That arc is one of the most legible in film history, and each film comments on the last.

Week 11 · Nov 2–8

  • Rome, Open City (1945, Rossellini · 103 min) — shot in the months right after the Germans left Rome, partly on scrounged film stock, in a city that hadn’t yet been repaired.
  • Bicycle Thieves (1948, De Sica · 89 min) — the neorealist thesis in its clearest form: no stars, real locations, a plot that could be a footnote.
  • Optional: Umberto D. (1952, De Sica · 89 min)

Week 12 · Nov 9–15

  • L’Avventura (1960, Antonioni · 143 min) — a woman disappears and the film loses interest in finding her. Booed at Cannes; then it wasn’t.
  • Il Sorpasso (1962, Risi · 105 min) — the comedy that diagnoses exactly the same postwar emptiness, and is a delight. The pairing is the lesson.
  • Optional: L’Eclisse (1962, Antonioni · 126 min)

Week 13 · Nov 16–22

  • 8½ (1963, Fellini · 138 min) — a director who can’t make his film, making it.
  • The Conformist (1970, Bertolucci · 113 min) — Storaro’s cinematography is the reason half of New Hollywood looks the way it does. Watch it and then rewatch bits of The Godfather in Unit 8.
  • Optional: La Dolce Vita (1960, Fellini · 174 min)

Read: Criterion’s essays on L’Avventura and 8½.

Unit 5 · The French New Wave and What It Stole

Weeks 14–16 · 23 Nov – 13 Dec 2026

The trick with the New Wave is that it’s usually taught as a rupture. It’s more useful as theft: a group of critics who loved American genre film and remade it with handheld cameras and no money. Each week here pairs a New Wave film with the thing it was stealing from or handing off to.

Week 14 · Nov 23–29

  • Bob le Flambeur (1956, Melville · 98 min) — the low-budget location-shot crime film that showed the younger critics it could be done.
  • Breathless (1960, Godard · 90 min) — then the jump cuts. Having seen Melville first, you’ll see what’s new and what isn’t.
  • Optional: The Killing (1956, Kubrick · 84 min)

Week 15 · Nov 30 – Dec 6

  • Cléo from 5 to 7 (1962, Varda · 90 min) — near-real-time, and the best film of the movement by some distance.
  • Jules and Jim (1962, Truffaut · 105 min)
  • Optional: Band of Outsiders (1964, Godard · 95 min)

Week 16 · Dec 7–13

  • Contempt (1963, Godard · 103 min) — Godard with money, widescreen, and Brigitte Bardot; a film about the impossibility of the film it’s inside.
  • Le Samouraï (1967, Melville · 105 min) — where the influence loops back. Almost no dialogue; every frame is composition.
  • Optional: Pierrot le Fou (1965, Godard · 110 min)

Read: Truffaut, “A Certain Tendency of the French Cinema” (1954) — the manifesto, and short.

Unit 6 · Japan

Weeks 17–20 · 4 – 31 Jan 2027

The deepest single national tradition on this list and the biggest single gap for most self-taught viewers. Ozu first, because his style is so rigorously constrained that once you notice the rules, every other director’s choices become visible by contrast.

Week 17 · Jan 4–10 — Ozu

  • Late Spring (1949, Ozu · 108 min)
  • Tokyo Story (1953, Ozu · 136 min) — the camera sits three feet off the floor and almost never moves. Count how often he cuts to an empty room.
  • Optional: Early Summer (1951, Ozu · 125 min)

Week 18 · Jan 11–17 — Kurosawa, at length

  • Seven Samurai (1954, Kurosawa · 207 min) — take it as the week’s whole assignment; split across two nights if you like.
  • Optional: Yojimbo (1961, Kurosawa · 110 min) — and note that Leone remade it as A Fistful of Dollars

Week 19 · Jan 18–24

  • Ugetsu (1953, Mizoguchi · 96 min) — long takes, ghost story, and camera movement as moral commentary.
  • Harakiri (1962, Kobayashi · 133 min) — the samurai film turned into an indictment of the samurai code. Formally immaculate.
  • Optional: Sansho the Bailiff (1954, Mizoguchi · 124 min)

Week 20 · Jan 25–31

  • High and Low (1963, Kurosawa · 143 min) — a kidnapping procedural that splits cleanly into two formal halves. The first hour is nearly a filmed play.
  • Woman in the Dunes (1964, Teshigahara · 147 min) — sand, in extreme close-up, for two and a half hours, and it works.
  • Optional: Tokyo Drifter (1966, Suzuki · 83 min)

Read: Bordwell on Ozu (his blog has years of it); Criterion’s Tokyo Story essay.

Unit 7 · The Northern Interior

Weeks 21–23 · 1 – 21 Feb 2027

The demanding stretch, placed here on purpose — by February you’ll have the patience and the vocabulary for it. Bergman, Dreyer, Tarkovsky: films that are about faces, silence, doubt, and duration. Long, slow, and among the highest-value viewing on the list. If any unit is worth the extension film, it’s this one.

Week 21 · Feb 1–7

  • Wild Strawberries (1957, Bergman · 91 min) — the warmest entry point.
  • The Seventh Seal (1957, Bergman · 96 min) — much funnier than its reputation.
  • Optional: Winter Light (1963, Bergman · 81 min)

Week 22 · Feb 8–14

  • Persona (1966, Bergman · 83 min) — the film breaks, literally, in the middle. Two faces merge. Nobody has satisfactorily explained it and that’s fine.
  • Ordet (1955, Dreyer · 126 min) — the final scene is one of the few in cinema that genuinely cannot be described.
  • Optional: Cries and Whispers (1972, Bergman · 91 min)

Week 23 · Feb 15–21

  • Stalker (1979, Tarkovsky · 161 min) — three men walk very slowly toward a room. It is either the most boring thing you’ve seen or it isn’t.
  • Solaris (1972, Tarkovsky · 167 min) — a long week; drop to one film if you need to and take Stalker.
  • Optional: Andrei Rublev (1966, Tarkovsky · 183 min) — Tarkovsky’s preferred cut; the 205-min Passion According to Andrei also circulates

Read: Tarkovsky, Sculpting in Time (excerpts); Criterion essay on Ordet.

Unit 8 · New Hollywood

Weeks 24–27 · 22 Feb – 21 Mar 2027

Reward for Unit 7, and not a lesser unit for it. American directors who had absorbed exactly the European films you just watched, handed studio money, briefly, before the blockbuster closed the window. You’ll now recognize the borrowings — the Antonioni in the drift, the Bertolucci in the light.

Week 24 · Feb 22–28

  • Bonnie and Clyde (1967, Penn · 111 min) — French New Wave technique smuggled into a studio picture. Truffaut and Godard were both approached to direct it.
  • The Graduate (1967, Nichols · 106 min)
  • Optional: The Wild Bunch (1969, Peckinpah · 145 min)

Week 25 · Mar 1–7

  • The Godfather (1972, Coppola · 175 min) — watch Gordon Willis’s lighting, having just seen The Conformist.
  • Chinatown (1974, Polanski · 130 min) — noir rebuilt with everything Unit 2 taught you.
  • Optional: The Godfather Part II (1974, Coppola · 202 min)

Week 26 · Mar 8–14

  • Nashville (1975, Altman · 160 min) — twenty-four characters, overlapping sound, no center. The anti-Godfather.
  • Taxi Driver (1976, Scorsese · 114 min)
  • Optional: McCabe & Mrs. Miller (1971, Altman · 120 min)

Week 27 · Mar 15–21

  • Days of Heaven (1978, Malick · 94 min) — shot almost entirely at magic hour. Nestor Almendros, twenty minutes of usable light a day.
  • Apocalypse Now (1979, Coppola · 147 min) — the theatrical cut. The window closing.
  • Optional: The Conversation (1974, Coppola · 113 min)

Read: Pauline Kael on Nashville; then Peter Biskind, Easy Riders, Raging Bulls (gossipy, fast, and genuinely useful context).

Unit 9 · Hong Kong and Taiwan

Weeks 28–30 · 22 Mar – 11 Apr 2027

The single most rewarding blind spot on your list. Wong Kar-wai and Edward Yang were making, in the 1990s, some of the best films anyone was making anywhere, and they’re barely taught outside film departments.

Week 28 · Mar 22–28 — Wong Kar-wai

  • Days of Being Wild (1990, Wong · 94 min)
  • In the Mood for Love (2000, Wong · 98 min) — the film most often named the best of this century. Repetition, blocked sightlines, and a story told almost entirely in what’s withheld.
  • Optional: Happy Together (1997, Wong · 96 min)

Week 29 · Mar 29 – Apr 4

  • Yi Yi (2000, Yang · 173 min) — a family across three generations; possibly the most humane film on this syllabus.
  • Hard Boiled (1992, Woo · 128 min) — the counterweight, and a reminder that this industry also produced the most kinetic action filmmaking on earth.
  • Optional: A City of Sadness (1989, Hou Hsiao-hsien · 157 min)

Week 30 · Apr 5–11

  • A Brighter Summer Day (1991, Yang · 237 min) — the week’s single assignment. Two nights, and worth every minute.
  • Optional: Chungking Express rewatch — you’ll see a completely different film than you did in Week 2

Read: Criterion essays on In the Mood for Love and A Brighter Summer Day; Bordwell’s Planet Hong Kong (excerpts).

Unit 10 · Off the Usual Axis

Weeks 31–33 · 12 Apr – 2 May 2027

India, Iran, Senegal. Traditions that developed largely outside the Hollywood/Europe conversation, and the correction to a canon that mostly wasn’t looking.

Week 31 · Apr 12–18 — Satyajit Ray

  • Pather Panchali (1955, Ray · 125 min) — shot on weekends over three years with a mostly amateur crew.
  • Charulata (1964, Ray · 117 min)
  • Optional: Aparajito (1956, Ray · 110 min)

Week 32 · Apr 19–25 — Iran

  • Close-Up (1990, Kiarostami · 98 min) — a man impersonates a film director; Kiarostami films the trial and then restages it with the real people. The documentary/fiction boundary simply dissolves. You will have opinions about the epistemology.
  • Taste of Cherry (1997, Kiarostami · 99 min)
  • Optional: Where Is the Friend’s House? (1987, Kiarostami · 83 min)

Week 33 · Apr 26 – May 2 — Senegal

  • Black Girl (1966, Sembène · 65 min) — widely considered the first sub-Saharan African feature to reach international audiences.
  • Touki Bouki (1973, Mambéty · 88 min) — formally wilder than almost anything in Unit 5.
  • Optional: Xala (1975, Sembène · 123 min)

Read: Criterion’s essay on Close-Up; the Black Girl liner notes.

Unit 11 · Germany and the Political Film

Weeks 34–36 · 3 – 23 May 2027

Films that treat history as an unresolved argument. The New German Cinema directors were working directly against a national silence, and the political thrillers here were made close enough to their events to be dangerous.

Week 34 · May 3–9

  • Aguirre, the Wrath of God (1972, Herzog · 95 min) — shot on a stolen camera in the Peruvian jungle. The production stories are true.
  • Ali: Fear Eats the Soul (1974, Fassbinder · 93 min) — a remake of Sirk’s All That Heaven Allows, transposed to Munich and race.
  • Optional: All That Heaven Allows (1955, Sirk · 89 min) — watch it first if you can

Week 35 · May 10–16

  • The Battle of Algiers (1966, Pontecorvo · 121 min) — shot documentary-style; the opening title card insists no newsreel footage was used, because viewers assumed otherwise.
  • Z (1969, Costa-Gavras · 127 min)
  • Optional: The Marriage of Maria Braun (1979, Fassbinder · 120 min)

Week 36 · May 17–23

  • Wings of Desire (1987, Wenders · 128 min) — Berlin from above, in monochrome, just before the wall came down.
  • Night and Fog (1956, Resnais · 32 min) — the most-taught documentary in film curricula, and the one that established how the form handles atrocity: present-tense color tracking shots over the empty camps, cut against black-and-white archive. Watch it before Come and See, not after.
  • Come and See (1985, Klimov · 142 min) — the hardest film on this syllabus by a wide margin, and unarguably a great one. Skip it if you’d rather; substitute The Lives of Others (2006, von Donnersmarck · 137 min).
  • Optional: Shoah (1985, Lanzmann · 566 min) — a long-term project rather than a week’s viewing

This is the heaviest week of the year emotionally, not in runtime. Give it space, and don’t stack it against a deadline.

Read: Criterion’s Battle of Algiers materials, including the notorious 2003 Pentagon screening.

Unit 12 · Cinema Now

Weeks 37–39 · 24 May – 13 Jun 2027

Post-1990, the canon still forming. You’ll now be able to see these as arguments with everything above rather than as standalone entertainments — which is the whole return on the previous nine months.

Week 37 · May 24–30

  • A Separation (2011, Farhadi · 123 min) — the best-constructed screenplay of its decade; no character is wrong.
  • 4 Months, 3 Weeks and 2 Days (2007, Mungiu · 113 min) — the Romanian New Wave long take at its most unbearable and most controlled.
  • Optional: The Lives of Others (2006, von Donnersmarck · 137 min)

Week 38 · May 31 – Jun 6

  • Memories of Murder (2003, Bong · 132 min)
  • Parasite (2019, Bong · 132 min) — watch them together and the sixteen-year throughline is obvious: the same director, the same class rage, vastly more control.
  • Optional: Burning (2018, Lee Chang-dong · 148 min)

Week 39 · Jun 7–13

  • Spirited Away (2001, Miyazaki · 125 min)
  • Portrait of a Lady on Fire (2019, Sciamma · 122 min) — a film about looking, made by someone who has clearly seen everything in Unit 4.
  • Optional: Beau Travail (1999, Denis · 92 min)

Read: Sight & Sound’s 2022 Greatest Films poll and the essays around it — useful now that you can argue with it.

Unit 13 · Capstone — Cinema as Pure Form

Weeks 40–42 · 14 Jun – 4 Jul 2027

Three weeks of films that are difficult only without context. You now have the context. This unit would have been miserable in September and should be exhilarating in June — which is itself the demonstration that the whole approach worked.

Week 40 · Jun 14–20

  • 2001: A Space Odyssey (1968, Kubrick · 149 min)
  • Playtime (1967, Tati · 124 min) — shot in 70mm on a purpose-built city that bankrupted him. Two films from the same two years about human beings dwarfed by their own architecture.
  • La Jetée (1962, Marker · 28 min) — told almost entirely in still photographs: a film about time and memory that questions whether cinema needs motion at all. Sitting beside Kubrick’s vision of the future, it’s the better companion piece of the two.
  • Optional: Solaris rewatch, or Stalker if you skipped it

Week 41 · Jun 21–27

  • Mulholland Drive (2001, Lynch · 147 min)
  • Persona rewatch — the Bergman film Lynch is unmistakably working from. Forty minutes in, the connection is not subtle.
  • Optional: Beau Travail (1999, Denis · 92 min)

Week 42 · Jun 28 – Jul 4

  • Jeanne Dielman, 23 quai du Commerce, 1080 Bruxelles (1975, Akerman · 201 min) — a woman does housework in real time for three hours. Voted the greatest film ever made in the 2022 Sight & Sound poll, to widespread outrage. The week’s single assignment, and the right film to end on: it will either vindicate the whole year or give you something to be productively furious about.
  • Optional: rewatch Citizen Kane from Week 1. It’s a good way to measure the distance.

Slack built in

Three weeks are single-film weeks by design (18, 30, 42). The holiday break is three weeks of nothing. Extensions are always optional. If you finish early, the extension list alone is another 40 films.

The four weeks carrying an extra title (9, 10, 36, 40) add 104 minutes between them across the whole year — roughly one additional feature, spread over ten months. They exist to close two gaps that every academic film course treats as essential and this plan originally skipped entirely: German Expressionism, and non-narrative film (avant-garde and documentary). Four short films is a cheap price for both.

Essays to read after each film — 24 Aug 2026 – 4 Jul 2027

Organized by unit, week, and film, matching the syllabus. Optional-extension films are marked (optional).

The rule from the syllabus still holds: watch first, write your three or four sentences, then read. These are here so that when you want a second opinion you have one within a click, not so that you arrive at the film pre-interpreted.

About the sources. Criterion’s Current essays are free and are usually the best single starting point for any film in their collection. Roger Ebert’s Great Movies essays are free, short, and unpretentious. Senses of Cinema is free, longer, and more academic — its Great Directors entries are the fastest way to place a film in a career. David Bordwell’s Observations on Film Art is the formalist option and the one closest to the vocabulary in Film Art; where he’s written on a film it’s usually the most technically illuminating thing available. Scraps from the Loft reprints out-of-print criticism (Kael, Sarris, Kauffmann) that is otherwise hard to find legally online. Items marked (paywalled) need a subscription; a few need library access.

A note on links. Every URL here was loaded and checked against the essay it claims to be, as of August 2026. If one eventually 404s, searching the essay title plus the site name will usually find it — Criterion occasionally renumbers.

Unit 1 · The Grammar

Week 1 · Aug 24–30

Citizen Kane (1941, Welles)

  • Citizen Kane: The Once and Future Kane — Criterion. The film’s critical fortunes, from Sarris vs. Kael onward.
  • Citizen Kane — Roger Ebert, Great Movies. The standard appreciation of deep focus and “emotional chronology.”
  • Welles at 101, KANE at 75 or thereabouts — David Bordwell. What Welles actually invented versus what he inherited — the corrective to the origin myth.

Rear Window (1954, Hitchcock)

The gaps are not where you’d expect. Criterion covers Units 6, 7, 9, and 10 — Japan, the Bergman/Dreyer/Tarkovsky stretch, Hong Kong/Taiwan, and India/Iran/Senegal — completely, 32 for 32. That is precisely the material that’s hard to find anywhere else, and it’s the strongest argument for the subscription.

Where Criterion is thin is American studio cinema: Unit 2 (1 of 8) and Unit 8 (0 of 8). But those films are the easiest in the world to find — most are free on Kanopy or Tubi, and the rest rent for $3.99. So the coverage gap and the difficulty gap run in opposite directions, which is lucky.

What to actually subscribe to

Criterion Channel — yes, clearly. $10.99/mo. Carries 47 core films including every single film in four units.

A library card for Kanopy — yes, and it’s free. Kanopy alone covers 18 of the 33 Criterion gaps, including most of Unit 3 (silents) and a good chunk of Unit 8. Check whether Berkeley’s library login works, and whether your public library card does too — many people have access through both, and the borrowing limits are separate.

HBO Max — optional. It duplicates a lot of Criterion. It’s uniquely required for only four core films: Singin’ in the Rain (W7), Spirited Away (W39), Portrait of a Lady on Fire (W39), and 2001 (W40). All four are also rentable. Skip it, or subscribe for one month in June 2027.

Everything else: rent as you go. Budget roughly $45–55 in rentals for the whole year (about 12 films at $3.99). Cheaper than a second subscription.

The one real problem film

Nashville (1975), Week 26 — no subscription, no free option, and no rental. Purchase only, about $4.99 digital. It has drifted in and out of streaming for years. Options: buy it, check the library for a physical copy, or substitute McCabe & Mrs. Miller (the listed extension for that week, and a fine Altman in its own right).

Important caveat

The Criterion Channel rotates its catalogue monthly. These 47 are on today. Units 6 through 13 don’t come up until January–July 2027, so some will have cycled off and others cycled on by then. Re-check a month ahead of each unit. The rotation is usually generous in both directions — Criterion adds ~45 titles a month.

Three species of course

The eleven sort cleanly into three types, and they are not doing the same thing at all.

1. The chronological history survey (Utah, DePaul, UNT, MIT 21L.011). Marches through periods in order, one film per movement, tethered to a textbook — Cook or Thompson & Bordwell. Usually split into two semesters at 1945. The film is an example of a period; you watch one Neorealist film, not three.

2. The formal-concepts analysis course (UNC Wilmington, Florida, Toronto). Organized by technique — mise-en-scène, then cinematography, then editing, then sound — with films chosen to demonstrate the technique regardless of era. Almost universally assigns Bordwell & Thompson’s Film Art. Jumps freely from 1924 to 2001 in a single week.

3. The world-cinema survey (Eckerd, UC Davis, Houston). One film per national tradition, explicitly framed against Hollywood as the default. Eckerd’s course contains zero US films, by design.

The sabbatical plan is a fourth thing. It’s closest to type 3 in content and type 2 in Unit 1, but its organizing bet — 3–4 films inside one director or movement so that style becomes legible through repetition — is something none of these eleven courses does. They can’t: a 15-week term with one screening slot can’t spend four weeks on Bergman. That constraint is exactly what a sabbatical removes.

Where they diverge — the honest finding

Only 35 of the plan’s 80 films appear in any of the eleven syllabi. 45 appear in none.

That sounds damning and mostly isn’t. It’s an artifact of the depth strategy: these courses screen one Kurosawa, and the plan screens four. Seven Samurai shows up in the syllabi; Ugetsu, Harakiri, High and Low, and Woman in the Dunes don’t. Same for Bergman (they screen Persona; the plan adds three more), Wong Kar-wai, Antonioni, Fellini, Tarkovsky, and all of New Hollywood.

So the divergence is largely deliberate and defensible. But three gaps are not.

Gap 1 — Avant-garde and experimental film: 15 titles across the syllabi, zero in the plan

This is the biggest real omission. Nearly every formal-analysis course devotes a full unit to it: Meshes of the Afternoon (4 syllabi), Un Chien Andalou, Ballet mécanique, Mothlight, Kustom Kar Kommandos, Scorpio Rising, A Movie, La Jetée. These are taught because they isolate what narrative films do invisibly — and most are under 30 minutes, so the cost of adding them is trivial.

Gap 2 — Documentary: 9 titles across the syllabi, zero in the plan

Night and Fog (32 min), Land Without Bread, Triumph of the Will, The Hour of the Furnaces, Dark Days. The plan treats “film” as “narrative feature” throughout, which is a real narrowing.

Gap 3 — Early cinema and German Expressionism

The plan starts in 1924 with Keaton. The syllabi spend 19% of screenings pre-1930 against the plan’s 8%, and they all begin with Lumière/Méliès/Porter shorts — The Great Train Robbery, A Trip to the Moon. More pointedly, German Expressionism is missing entirely from the plan’s core: Caligari is in 3 syllabi, Metropolis in 3, Nosferatu and The Last Laugh in one each. The plan has Murnau’s Sunrise, which is a Hollywood film.

Taken together, non-narrative and pre-1915 material is 24% of everything the eleven courses screen and 0% of the plan.

Where the plan is ahead of the academic courses

Worth saying: on Asian cinema the plan is substantially deeper than any of the eleven. Yang’s A Brighter Summer Day and Yi Yi, Kobayashi’s Harakiri, Wong’s Days of Being Wild, Hou — none appear in any syllabus here. Same for the Iranian and Senegalese units beyond the two obligatory titles. Several of these courses handle “world cinema” with a single film per continent.

What I’d actually change

Five suggestions, roughly in order of payoff. All are cheap — mostly short films slotted into existing weeks.

  1. Add Meshes of the Afternoon (14 min) and Un Chien Andalou (16 min) to Week 10, alongside Man with a Movie Camera. That week is already about non-narrative form; it becomes an avant-garde unit for 30 extra minutes.

  2. Add The Cabinet of Dr. Caligari (1920) to Week 9, replacing or joining The Passion of Joan of Arc. This fixes the German Expressionism hole, and Expressionist lighting is the thing Unit 2’s noir week is descended from — it makes Week 6 land harder.

  3. Add Night and Fog (32 min) to Week 36, next to Come and See. Documentary enters the plan in the one unit where it belongs thematically, and it’s the most-taught documentary in the sample.

  4. Add La Jetée (28 min) to Week 40, with 2001 and Playtime. It’s in 2 syllabi, it’s 28 minutes, and a photo-roman about time and memory sitting beside Kubrick is a genuinely good pairing.

  5. Consider swapping in North by Northwest for Rear Window in Week 1, or adding it. Hitchcock appears across these syllabi more than any other director, and North by Northwest is the one they teach for formal analysis — the crop-duster sequence is the standard editing example.

Items 1–4 add about 90 minutes of total runtime across the year and close the two structural gaps. That’s the whole fix.
